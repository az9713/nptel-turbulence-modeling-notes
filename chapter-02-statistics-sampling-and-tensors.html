2. Statistics, sampling, and Cartesian tensors

A velocity probe in the channel records one hundred values. Their average appears steady to two decimal places. That is insufficient evidence that the true mean is known to that precision: adjacent observations may describe the same slowly passing structure. Meanwhile, two signals can share a mean and variance yet transport different momentum. A statistical description must specify its averaging law, the dependence between samples, and the information retained in each moment.

Lessons 2–5 provide the dice experiment, ensemble and time averages, fluctuations and moments, sampling convergence, covariance and correlation, two-point statistics, and Cartesian notation. Definitions and qualifications are developed independently. In particular, finite-sample deviations about their own mean sum to zero exactly, stationarity does not imply ergodicity, and zero covariance does not imply independence or absence of turbulence.

2.1 A sample mean is an uncertain estimate L2 · 00:15

Let \(X\) be a scalar random variable, a numerical function of a realization of an experiment. For a finite outcome set with values \(x_r\) and probabilities \(p_r\ge0\), where \(\sum_rp_r=1\), its expectation is \(\mathbb E[X]=\sum_rp_rx_r\). For a continuous variable with probability density \(f(x)\), it is \(\int x f(x)\,dx\), when that integral is absolutely convergent. A theoretical mean is a property of the stated probability law. It is not defined by having a particularly long measurement file.

For a fair six-sided die, \(p_r=1/6\) for \(r=1,\ldots,6\), giving mean \(m=3.5\). A sample consists of observed values \(X_1,\ldots,X_N\), and its arithmetic mean is \(\widehat m_N=N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^NX_n\). Before the observations are made, this mean is itself a random variable. Another preparation can give a different value. After observing the sample, its computed value is fixed, but its error relative to the unknown population mean is not generally known.

The source records a ten-value row \(5,6,1,3,4,6,1,6,4,1\), whose sum is 37 and mean 3.7. Under the fair-die model, its estimation error is \(3.7-3.5=0.2\). Fairness is an assumption for this analysis, not something ten throws prove. A separate captioned row has an apparent arithmetic mismatch with its spoken mean; no unverified correction to that board example is needed here.

Write \(m=\mathbb E[X]\), \(X'=X-m\), and define variance \(\sigma^2=\mathbb E[(X')^2]\), with standard deviation \(\sigma\). Expectation is linear, so for identically distributed samples \(\mathbb E[\widehat m_N]=m\), whether or not they are independent. Independence means that their joint probabilities factor into the individual probabilities; it is stronger than vanishing pairwise covariance. If the samples are independent and have finite variance, expanding the square of the mean error gives

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\widehat m_N) ={1\over N^2}\sum_{r,s=1}^N \mathbb E[(X_r-m)(X_s-m)]={\sigma^2\over N}. \tag{2.1} \]

For different indices, independence makes the expectation a product of zero means; only \(N\) diagonal terms remain. Thus the standard deviation of the mean is \(\sigma/\sqrt N\). It measures the spread of repeated estimates, not the scatter of individual observations. For the fair die, \(\sigma^2=35/12\), so ten independent throws have mean-estimator standard deviation \(\sqrt{35/120}=0.54006\). A change of only 0.01 between successive running averages does not establish 0.01 accuracy.

2.2 Exact averaging identities and convergence L2 · 14:06

For the true mean, \(\mathbb E[X']=\mathbb E[X-m]=m-m=0\). An expectation of a deterministic quantity returns that quantity, so \(\mathbb E[\mathbb E[X]]=\mathbb E[X]\). These statements concern a fixed probability law, not the number of samples used to estimate it. Expectations and all required moments must exist before manipulating them.

There is also an exact finite-sample identity. Define sample deviations \(d_n=X_n-\widehat m_N\). Then \(\sum_nd_n=\sum_nX_n-N\widehat m_N=0\). It holds even when \(\widehat m_N\ne m\). For the ten throws above, deviations about 3.7 sum to zero, although the fair-die mean is 3.5. A zero average residual about an estimated mean therefore cannot certify that estimate. Conversely, a nonzero average relative to a separately estimated or prescribed mean may reflect estimation or preparation error, rather than a failure of the arithmetic operation.

The finite-variance independent-sample result (2.1) gives one precise convergence statement. For tolerance \(a\gt0\), split the expectation of the squared mean error according to whether its magnitude is at least \(a\). On that event its square is at least \(a^2\), so

\[ \Pr(|\widehat m_N-m|\ge a) \le{\mathbb E[(\widehat m_N-m)^2]\over a^2} ={\sigma^2\over Na^2}. \tag{2.2} \]

This is Chebyshev's bound, derived here for the sample mean. The probability tends to zero with \(N\) under the stated assumptions. The bound may be conservative; it does not say an individual running estimate must move monotonically toward the mean. Nor does it say independent sampling is necessary for every convergence theorem. Correlated processes need conditions appropriate to their dependence.

For the die and \(a=0.1\), a guaranteed bound of at most 0.05 requires \(N\ge(35/12)/(0.05\cdot0.1^2)\), or at least 5834 independent throws after rounding upward. This is a sufficient bound, not the smallest possible sample requirement for that known distribution. A realistic measurement plan also includes bias, calibration, and changing conditions; more samples of the wrong preparation do not remove those errors.

2.3 Ensemble, time, and phase averages L3 · 00:15

A random velocity field \(u_i(\mathbf x,t,\omega)\) depends on position, time, and realization \(\omega\). Its ensemble mean \(U_i(\mathbf x,t)=\mathbb E[u_i(\mathbf x,t,\omega)]\) averages across preparations at fixed \(\mathbf x,t\). It can remain a function of time. Repeated engine cycles, for example, can have a reproducible phase-dependent temperature while individual cycles fluctuate about it.

A time average over duration \(\mathcal T\) at a fixed probe location is \(\mathcal T^{-1}\int_0^{\mathcal T}u_i(t,\omega)\,dt\). It uses one realization across times rather than different realizations at one time. Strict stationarity means that every finite joint distribution is unchanged by a common time shift. Second-order stationarity requires a constant mean and a covariance depending only on time separation, with finite second moments. A constant mean alone is weaker than either complete statistical invariance or the second-order condition.

Ergodicity for a quantity means that its long-time average equals the corresponding ensemble expectation, under the relevant almost-sure or other convergence statement. Stationarity does not ensure it. Let \(Z\) be a random sign, equally likely \(\pm1\), and set \(X(t)=Z\) for every time. The process is stationary and has ensemble mean zero. Each realization's time average is its fixed sign, never zero. No waiting time within that realization generates an independent preparation.

For a periodic mean, indiscriminate time averaging can erase a physical signal. If \(X(t,\omega)=A\cos(\Omega t)+\eta(t,\omega)\), with amplitude \(A\), angular frequency \(\Omega\), and zero-mean random disturbance \(\eta\), the ensemble mean is \(A\cos(\Omega t)\). Averaging over many complete periods may instead give zero. Phase averaging compares repeated cycles at the same phase of the forcing; it can retain the periodic mean while reducing random cycle variability. Sampling must be matched to the question, rather than assuming all averages estimate the same object.

2.4 Separate a field from its statistical departures L3 · 08:09

Use \(u_i=U_i+u'_i\) for velocity and \(p=P+p'\) for pressure. The prime denotes departure from the ensemble mean, not the entire unsteady part of a record. A time-dependent \(U_i\) is possible, and a deterministic periodic response belongs to that mean if it repeats across the ensemble. This distinction matters when interpreting an oscillating boundary layer.

For scalar velocity fluctuation \(u'\), the fluctuation root-mean-square value is \(u'_{\rm rms}=\sqrt{\mathbb E[(u')^2]}\), in m/s. It equals the standard deviation of the instantaneous velocity. The RMS of the full velocity differs: expanding \(u=U+u'\) gives \(\mathbb E[u^2]=U^2+\mathbb E[(u')^2]\). A mean speed of 10 m/s with fluctuation variance 1 m²/s² has full RMS speed \(\sqrt{101}=10.0499\) m/s and fluctuation RMS 1 m/s.

Subtracting a varying ensemble mean removes its deterministic time dependence. Subtracting one constant from a periodic record does not. The remaining periodic component then inflates the estimated fluctuation variance. For a uniformly sampled cosine of amplitude \(A\) over complete cycles, its variance contribution is \(A^2/2\), since the cycle average of \(\cos^2\) is \(1/2\). A variance is interpretable only after specifying the mean removed.

In the channel, component variances describe fluctuation strengths in streamwise, wall-normal, and spanwise directions. They are nonnegative. A zero variance for one component means that component equals its mean with probability one; it does not eliminate fluctuations in the other components. Positive scalar variance by itself also does not identify turbulence, because coherent stochastic forcing or measurement noise can fluctuate.

2.5 Higher moments retain different information L3 · 17:35

The central moment of integer order \(r\) is \(M_r=\mathbb E[(X-m)^r]\), when it exists. Variance is \(M_2\). For \(M_2\gt0\), skewness is \(M_3/M_2^{3/2}\), and flatness, also called kurtosis here, is \(M_4/M_2^2\). These ratios are dimensionless. Bare third and fourth moments have cubed and fourth-power units, so they must not be called the normalized coefficients without stating the normalization. Excess kurtosis would subtract three; this edition uses flatness without that subtraction.

Consider distribution A, equally likely values \(-1,+1\). It has mean zero, variance one, third moment zero, and flatness one. Distribution B takes zero with probability \(2/3\), and each of \(-\sqrt3,+\sqrt3\) with probability \(1/6\). Its variance is \(2(1/6)3=1\), third moment zero by symmetry, and fourth moment \(2(1/6)9=3\). Thus it has the same mean and variance but flatness three. B spends more probability at zero and includes rarer larger excursions.

For an asymmetric example, let \(X=-1\) with probability \(2/3\) and \(X=2\) with probability \(1/3\). The mean is zero, variance is \(2\), and third moment is \(-2/3+8/3=2\). Skewness is \(2/2^{3/2}=1/\sqrt2\), positive because the less frequent positive excursion is larger. These finite distributions are teaching constructions, not turbulence measurements. Even matching several moments does not generally identify a whole distribution, its temporal dependence, or its spatial organization.

Higher moments require stronger integrability and can be harder to estimate from finite data. A rare event may contribute little to a mean yet dominate a fourth moment. A seemingly stable mean therefore provides no guarantee that flatness or a tail probability has converged. The later energy and stress equations reveal which combinations of moments transport particular physical quantities.

2.6 Estimate variability without mistaking centering for accuracy L4 · 00:15

A sample variance about the sample mean is \(s_N^2=N^{-1}\sum_n(X_n-\widehat m_N)^2\). It is a useful finite-data second moment but is not unbiased for population variance under independent identical sampling. To see why, expand around the true mean:

\[ \sum_n(X_n-\widehat m_N)^2 =\sum_n(X_n-m)^2-N(\widehat m_N-m)^2. \tag{2.3} \]

The cross term simplifies because \(\sum_n(X_n-m)=N(\widehat m_N-m)\). Taking expectations and applying (2.1) gives \(N\sigma^2-N(\sigma^2/N)=(N-1)\sigma^2\). Therefore \(\mathbb E[s_N^2]=(N-1)\sigma^2/N\). Dividing the squared deviations by \(N-1\), for \(N\gt1\), removes this bias under those assumptions.

The denominator correction does not make correlated samples independent, remove instrumentation bias, or guarantee small uncertainty. Estimator choice must match its intended target. A covariance matrix formed by averaging sample outer products with denominator \(N\) is positive semidefinite and describes that empirical distribution exactly. An unbiased population estimator answers a different question. For large independent samples the difference may be small; for short records or strong dependence it deserves explicit treatment.

The source suggests waiting for an eddy to leave a probe's region of influence before taking another sample. That is a physical motivation for examining correlation time. It is not a universal rule that all closely spaced observations are useless. Retaining a well-resolved time record and accounting for its dependence can be more informative than discarding samples. We first need a precise measure of that dependence.

2.7 Covariance describes joint variation L4 · 14:16

For velocity components with zero-mean departures \(u'_i\), define the single-point covariance \(R_{ij}=\mathbb E[u'_iu'_j]\), in m²/s². “Single point” means both factors use the same position and time, with averaging over realizations. Symmetry \(R_{ij}=R_{ji}\) follows from scalar multiplication, giving six independent entries. For positive component variances, define the dimensionless coefficient \(c_{ij}=R_{ij}/\sqrt{R_{ii}R_{jj}}\), with no summation in this coefficient definition.

The covariance is positive semidefinite: for any deterministic vector \(\mathbf a\), \(a_iR_{ij}a_j=\mathbb E[(a_iu'_i)^2]\ge0\), with repeated Cartesian indices summed. In two components, minimizing \(\mathbb E[(u'_1-\lambda u'_2)^2]\) over real \(\lambda\), when \(R_{22}\gt0\), gives \(R_{12}^2\le R_{11}R_{22}\). Hence \(|c_{12}|\le1\). If a variance is zero, that component is constant with probability one and its covariances vanish; the normalized coefficient is undefined rather than zero.

A constructed ensemble connects these statistics to the channel. Let \(X,Y\) be independent equally likely signs. Set \(u'=2X\) m/s and \(v'=(-X/2+\sqrt3\,Y/2)\) m/s. Since \(\mathbb E[X]=\mathbb E[Y]=\mathbb E[XY]=0\), their variances are \(4\) and \(1\) m²/s². Their covariance is \(-1\) m²/s² and correlation \(-1/2\). A negative coefficient describes a statistical tendency, not a rule that every upward event must be slow. The interactive covariance construction varies the coefficient while preserving the two variances.

Zero covariance does not imply independence. Take a scalar \(X\) uniform on \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and let \(Y=X^2\). Then \(\mathbb E[X]=0\), \(\mathbb E[Y]=2/3\), and \(\mathbb E[XY]=\mathbb E[X^3]=0\), so their covariance is zero. Yet observing \(Y=0\) determines \(X=0\), while observing \(X=1\) determines \(Y=1\). They are dependent. Zero linear correlation also does not prove the absence of turbulence; symmetry can force some cross covariances to vanish while variances remain nonzero.

2.8 Correlation measures time and spatial dependence L5 · 00:15

To compare different points and times, define \(C_{ij}(\mathbf r,\tau;\mathbf x,t)=\mathbb E[u'_i(\mathbf x,t)u'_j(\mathbf x+\mathbf r,t+\tau)]\). Here \(\mathbf r\) is a spatial separation in metres and \(\tau\) a time lag in seconds. At zero separation and lag it reduces to \(R_{ij}(\mathbf x,t)\). In general, normalization uses the standard deviation at each of the two locations and times. Reusing only the variance at the reference time gives a coefficient bounded by one only under additional equal-variance conditions.

For a second-order stationary scalar record, write \(C(\tau)=\mathbb E[X'(t)X'(t+\tau)]\) and \(c(\tau)=C(\tau)/\sigma^2\), assuming \(\sigma^2\gt0\). Then \(c(0)=1\), \(|c(\tau)|\le1\), and for a real scalar stationary process \(c(-\tau)=c(\tau)\). The bound follows by the same two-variable variance argument as the single-point coefficient. Stationarity removes the absolute-time dependence, not all dependence between observations.

For \(N\) equally spaced samples with interval \(\Delta t\), let \(c_\ell=c(\ell\Delta t)\). Expand the variance of their arithmetic mean as in (2.1), now retaining off-diagonal terms. There are \(N-\ell\) pairs at each positive lag and the same number at its negative:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\widehat m_N) ={\sigma^2\over N}\Gamma_N,\qquad \Gamma_N=1+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1} \left(1-{\ell\over N}\right)c_\ell. \tag{2.4} \]

This finite-\(N\) identity needs second-order stationarity, not independent observations. When the variance is positive, define effective sample size \(N_{\rm eff}=N/\Gamma_N\). It is the independent-sample count giving the same variance of the mean, not a count of physically distinct eddies. Positive long-lived correlation commonly makes \(N_{\rm eff}\) smaller than \(N\). Negative correlations can reduce mean-estimator variance and make this precision-equivalent quantity larger than \(N\).

For the constructed correlation \(c_\ell=0.9^\ell\), one hundred samples give \(\Gamma_{100}\approx17.20005\) and \(N_{\rm eff}\approx5.81394\). With unit variance, the mean-estimator standard deviation is about \(0.41473\), rather than the independent-sample value \(0.1\). As \(N\) grows while the correlation law stays fixed, \(\Gamma_N\) tends to \(1+2(0.9/0.1)=19\). The finite-record factor and its large-\(N\) limit should not be interchanged silently.

Oliver, Malaya, Ulerich, and Moser, §II.A, pp. 6–8, develop correlated sampling-error estimation for DNS statistics and discuss how sampling error can obscure discretization studies. Their practical method estimates the unknown correlation with time-series models. Equation (2.4) here uses a prescribed exact correlation for a teaching check; it does not implement their estimator or certify the correlation of a real turbulent record.

The NIST sample autocorrelation definition uses equally spaced observations and normalizes a lagged product sum by the full zero-lag squared-deviation sum. Different finite-sample normalizations are possible. For data \(-1,0,1\), this convention gives lag-two coefficient \(-1/2\). Dividing the lag product by \(N-\ell\) while retaining the variance's denominator \(N\) instead gives \(-1.5\). That latter finite-sample estimate is not constrained like a true equal-variance correlation. Declare the estimator before interpreting its plot.

For a stationary process with integrable correlation, define integral time scale \(T_{\rm int}=\int_0^\infty c(\tau)\,d\tau\). A long continuous record of duration \(\mathcal T\) has mean variance approximately \(2\sigma^2T_{\rm int}/\mathcal T\), when the tail and finite-window correction are negligible. This follows from the exact double integral \(\mathcal T^{-2}\int_0^{\mathcal T}\int_0^{\mathcal T}C(t-s)\,dt\,ds=2\mathcal T^{-2}\int_0^{\mathcal T}(\mathcal T-\tau)C(\tau)\,d\tau\). The integrated tail matters, not just the first zero of an estimated correlation.

Spatial correlation similarly tests structure relative to a computational box. For the illustrative law \(c(r)=\exp(-|r|/L_c)\), \(L_c\) is a correlation length in metres. Requiring \(c(B/2)\le0.01\), where \(B\) is a proposed periodic box length, gives \(B\ge2L_c\log100\approx9.21L_c\). This is a criterion for that assumed correlation, not a universal domain-size rule. In a periodic box the correlation repeats; examining separation up to half the box and comparing larger domains is more informative than assuming the imposed period proves adequate size. Wall-normal inhomogeneity also prevents replacing every two-point covariance by a function of separation alone.

2.9 Cartesian tensor notation preserves geometry L5 · 09:30

Fix an orthonormal Cartesian basis. A scalar has one component; a vector has three. A second-order tensor has components \(B_{ij}\) and transforms under an orthogonal basis change \(Q\) as \(B^{\rm new}_{ij}=Q_{ia}Q_{jb}B_{ab}\), or \(B^{\rm new}=QBQ^{\mathsf T}\). Tensor order describes how many basis directions enter the transformation, whereas matrix rank describes the dimension of an image. A zero matrix can represent an order-two tensor of matrix rank zero.

A tensor of order \(r\) in three dimensions has \(3^r\) displayed components before symmetries are imposed, with one factor of \(Q\) per index in its transformation. A third velocity moment \(\mathbb E[u'_iu'_ju'_k]\) has 27 displayed components, but commutativity makes it fully symmetric in the three indices. Its independent components correspond to the ten unordered triples selected from three labels, not 27 independent fields. Storage count, transformation order, and independent information are different quantities.

In the Einstein convention, an index appearing twice in one term is summed from 1 to 3. An index appearing once is free and has the same meaning throughout the equation. Thus \(a_ib_i\) is a scalar sum, while \(B_{ij}a_j\) has free index \(i\) and gives a vector. In \(c_i=B_{ij}a_j\), expanding \(i=1\) gives \(c_1=B_{11}a_1+B_{12}a_2+B_{13}a_3\). Renaming the summed index \(j\) to an unused label leaves that sum unchanged. Renaming a free index must be done consistently on both sides.

An index must not appear three times in an Einstein term such as \(a_ib_ic_i\); write an explicit summation if a coordinatewise expression is intended. An explicit sum does not automatically make an expression a coordinate-invariant scalar. The contraction \(a_ib_i\) is invariant under orthogonal basis changes because \(\sum_iQ_{ia}Q_{ib}=\delta_{ab}\), the orthogonality identity. That cancellation explains why physical geometric statements need transformation rules as well as compact notation.

The Kronecker delta \(\delta_{ij}\) equals one for equal indices and zero otherwise. It acts as identity: \(\delta_{ij}a_j=a_i\), and \(\delta_{ii}=3\). In right-handed Cartesian bases, the Levi-Civita symbol \(\epsilon_{ijk}\) is \(+1\) for cyclic permutations of \(123\), \(-1\) for anticyclic permutations, and zero for repeated labels. It represents the oriented volume tensor under proper rotations. Reflections reverse orientation, so axial quantities such as curl need the corresponding sign change; restrict the unchanged-symbol convention to right-handed bases.

For the constructed covariance \(R=\begin{bmatrix}4&-1&0\\-1&1&0\\0&0&1/4\end{bmatrix}\), a ninety-degree rotation about the third axis with \(Q=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\) gives \(QRQ^{\mathsf T}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\1&4&0\\0&0&1/4\end{bmatrix}\). The cross component changes sign, but trace remains \(5.25\) m²/s² and positive semidefiniteness is preserved. A component sign is tied to axes; the physical covariance has not changed.

2.10 Differential operators and index checks L5 · 22:43

Write \(\partial_i=\partial/\partial x_i\), where \(x_i\) is the coordinate in metres. A scalar pressure field has gradient components \(\partial_ip\), in Pa/m. A velocity gradient has components \(B_{ij}=\partial_ju_i\), in s⁻¹: velocity component first, derivative direction second. Its trace \(\partial_iu_i\) is divergence, a scalar. In incompressible flow this divergence is zero, while individual gradients need not vanish.

Curl is \(\omega_i=\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_ju_k\), in s⁻¹. To expand the first component, set \(i=1\). All terms with a repeated label inside \(\epsilon\) vanish, leaving \(\omega_1=\partial_2u_3-\partial_3u_2\). The signs come from \(\epsilon_{123}=1\), \(\epsilon_{132}=-1\). The same expansion gives the other two components without a memorized determinant trick.

The Laplacian of velocity has components \(\partial_j\partial_ju_i\), in \(1/(\mathrm{m\,s})\). Its derivative index is summed twice while \(i\) remains free. Advective acceleration has components \(u_j\partial_ju_i\), in m/s², with the same free index but a different operator. Multiplying the Laplacian by kinematic viscosity, in m²/s, also gives acceleration units. A correct free-index pattern is necessary for an equation, but dimensional agreement is a separate check.

For a linear field \(\mathbf u=B\mathbf x\), with \(B=\begin{bmatrix}0&2&0\\0&0&3\\4&0&0\end{bmatrix}\) s⁻¹, divergence is the trace, zero. Curl is \((B_{32}-B_{23},B_{13}-B_{31},B_{21}-B_{12})=(-3,-4,-2)\) s⁻¹. Every second derivative is zero, so the Laplacian vanishes, although advection \(B^2\mathbf x\) generally does not. These are kinematic calculations; no pressure or boundary conditions have yet been chosen to make this a full flow solution.

2.11 Execute sampling and tensor checks

The standard-library program computes exact finite-distribution moments, verifies sample centering despite estimation error, calculates mean variance for the prescribed correlated sequence, and checks covariance rotation and a linear field's curl. Its short autocorrelation example makes the normalization choice visible. Inputs are explicit mathematical constructions, and output is printed without files or network effects. Assertions compare independently derived identities. The program is available as code/ch02_statistics.py.

"""Finite sampling, correlation and Cartesian tensor checks."""
from math import isclose, sqrt

sample = [5, 6, 1, 3, 4, 6, 1, 6, 4, 1]
mean = sum(sample) / len(sample)
residual = sum(value - mean for value in sample)
assert isclose(residual, 0., abs_tol=1e-12)
die_mean = sum(range(1, 7)) / 6
die_variance = sum((value - die_mean)**2 for value in range(1, 7)) / 6
assert isclose(die_variance, 35 / 12)
n, correlation = 100, .9
factor = 1 + 2 * sum((1 - lag / n) * correlation**lag
                     for lag in range(1, n))
effective = n / factor
sd_mean = sqrt(factor / n)
data = [-1., 0., 1.]
denominator = sum(value**2 for value in data)
lag_product = data[0] * data[2]
acf = lag_product / denominator
mixed_normalization = lag_product / (denominator / len(data))
assert acf == -.5 and mixed_normalization == -1.5


def matmul(left, right):
    return [[sum(left[i][k] * right[k][j] for k in range(3))
             for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]


def transpose(matrix):
    return [list(row) for row in zip(*matrix)]


cov = [[4., -1., 0.], [-1., 1., 0.], [0., 0., .25]]
rotation = [[0., -1., 0.], [1., 0., 0.], [0., 0., 1.]]
rotated = matmul(matmul(rotation, cov), transpose(rotation))
trace = sum(cov[i][i] for i in range(3))
assert isclose(sum(rotated[i][i] for i in range(3)), trace)
gradient = [[0., 2., 0.], [0., 0., 3.], [4., 0., 0.]]
curl = [gradient[2][1] - gradient[1][2],
        gradient[0][2] - gradient[2][0],
        gradient[1][0] - gradient[0][1]]
assert curl == [-3., -4., -2.]
assert sum(gradient[i][i] for i in range(3)) == 0
print(f'sample mean: {mean:.6f}')
print(f'sample-centered residual (floating point): {residual:.3e}')
print(f'fair-die mean, variance: {die_mean:.6f}, {die_variance:.6f}')
print(f'correlation factor: {factor:.8f}')
print(f'effective samples: {effective:.6f}')
print(f'unit-variance mean standard deviation: {sd_mean:.6f}')
print(f'lag-2 coefficient, mixed normalization: {acf:.1f}, '
      f'{mixed_normalization:.1f}')
print('rotated covariance:', rotated)
print(f'covariance trace: {trace:.6f}')
print('linear-field curl:', curl)

The correlation factor uses a known population law rather than fitting an uncertain law to a record. The sample-centered residual is algebraically zero; its tiny printed floating-point remainder is roundoff, not a population fluctuation. Finite exact examples can expose conceptual and programming mistakes, but cannot establish the convergence or statistical adequacy of an arbitrary turbulence dataset. For a real probe, calibration, stationarity, sampling duration, aliasing, and correlation estimation need their own evidence.

2.12 Problems and worked solutions

1. A centered but inaccurate estimate. For sample \(2,4,6\), compute the mean and deviations. If the population mean is stipulated to be 5, what is the estimation error? Does zero average deviation resolve it?

Solution

The mean is 4, with deviations \(-2,0,2\), summing to zero. The mean-estimation error is \(4-5=-1\). Centering about the sample mean is an algebraic identity and contains no comparison with the population mean. Deviations about 5 would instead be \(-3,-1,1\), with average \(-1\), equal to the estimator error. A zero residual about an estimate cannot establish that estimate's physical or statistical accuracy.

2. Fair-die variance and precision. Derive variance \(35/12\) for a fair die, then compare mean-estimator standard deviations for \(N=10\) and \(N=1000\) independent throws.

Solution

The mean square is \((1+4+9+16+25+36)/6=91/6\). Subtracting mean squared gives \(91/6-(7/2)^2=(182-147)/12=35/12\). Divide by \(N\) and take a square root: at ten samples the standard deviation is \(0.540062\), and at one thousand it is \(0.0540062\). A hundredfold increase in independent sample count gives a tenfold precision improvement. These are spreads of repeated means, not a guarantee that any particular sample lies within one such spread.

3. Same variance, different extremes. Verify the flatness values of distributions A and B in §2.5. Compare their probabilities of an absolute value exceeding 1.5.

Solution

A has second and fourth moments both one, hence flatness one. B has second moment \(2(1/6)3=1\), fourth moment \(2(1/6)9=3\), hence flatness three. A never exceeds 1.5 in magnitude. B exceeds it whenever \(X=\pm\sqrt3\), with total probability \(1/3\). Identical means and variances permit different tail behavior. This calculation does not imply a universal relation between one flatness value and every tail probability for arbitrary distributions.

4. Stationary but not ergodic. For \(X(t)=Z\), where \(Z=\pm1\) with equal probability, determine variance, autocorrelation, and variance of the average of \(N\) repeated times in one realization. Compare (2.4).

Solution

The mean is zero, variance one, and every time pair has product \(Z^2=1\), so \(c_\ell=1\). The average is \(Z\), with variance one regardless of \(N\). In (2.4), \(\Gamma_N=1+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}(1-\ell/N)=1+2[(N-1)-(N-1)/2]=N\). Thus \(\sigma^2\Gamma_N/N=1\) and \(N_{\rm eff}=1\). Arbitrarily long observation of a fixed sign does not sample the ensemble.

5. Independent is stronger than uncorrelated. In the example \(X\in\{-1,0,1\}\) uniformly and \(Y=X^2\), calculate \(\Pr(X=0,Y=0)\) and \(\Pr(X=0)\Pr(Y=0)\). Explain why their difference is decisive.

Solution

The joint event is exactly \(X=0\), so its probability is \(1/3\). The product of the marginal probabilities is \((1/3)(1/3)=1/9\). Independence would require equality for every such pair of events, and this one fails. Yet the covariance is zero by the odd symmetry of \(X^3\). A linear second-moment test can miss deterministic nonlinear dependence.

6. Count symmetric components. List the independent entries of the fully symmetric third velocity moment in three dimensions. Why would calling its 27 displayed entries independent overcount information?

Solution

Sort each index triple into nondecreasing order. The possibilities are \(111,112,113,122,123,133,222,223,233,333\), ten entries. Any permutation, such as \(231\) or \(312\), equals the \(123\) entry because scalar velocity factors commute. Tensor order remains three and a full unsymmetrized array still has 27 positions, but repeated equal values do not create additional statistical degrees of freedom. This matters when diagnosing a moment hierarchy's missing information.

7. Diagnose an index error. A proposed velocity equation contains \(u_i\partial_ju_i\) on the right and acceleration component \(a_i\) on the left, under Einstein summation. What is wrong? Write the advective acceleration with a consistent free index.

Solution

On the proposed right side, \(i\) appears twice and is summed, while \(j\) appears once and is free. It therefore gives a vector labeled by \(j\), not the component \(i\) on the left. Advective acceleration is \(u_j\partial_ju_i\): the velocity and derivative direction share summed index \(j\), and \(i\) remains the component being accelerated. Units alone would not catch this error because both expressions have acceleration units.

8. A geometric box criterion. Let \(L_c=0.1\) m in the illustrative exponential spatial correlation. Find a box length giving correlation at half the box at most 0.01. State two reasons this does not prove a DNS domain is adequate.

Solution

The condition \(\exp[-B/(2L_c)]\le0.01\) gives \(B\ge2(0.1)\log100=0.921034\) m. The criterion assumes the exponential correlation law and a selected component/direction; real turbulence may have longer tails, anisotropy, or other relevant structures. A periodic computation also changes admissible spatial modes and may alter the correlation being measured. Comparing larger domains and the quantities of interest is needed before claiming domain independence.

2.13 What a statistical record can establish

An average is a defined operation on a specified probability law or observation window. Precision comes from sample variability and dependence, not a small change between the final two running means. Covariance records joint second moments; higher moments and two-point statistics retain different information. Cartesian tensors preserve the geometric meaning of those objects while indices organize component calculations.

The examples use finite distributions, a prescribed stationary correlation, and linear velocity fields. They do not recover an unknown probe's spectrum or validate a turbulent-flow model. The correlated-mean variance identity is exact under its second-order assumptions, but practical estimation of its correlation sum is a separate problem. The next chapter applies the same index and averaging discipline to conservation laws before any turbulence closure is introduced.
