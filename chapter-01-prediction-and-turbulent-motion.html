1. Prediction, uncertainty, and turbulent motion

An air-handling system must deliver a specified flow through a channel. Its designer needs a pressure drop and a power requirement, rather than the path of every fluid parcel. Yet the pressure drop depends on momentum exchange by motions far smaller than the channel. A useful calculation must decide which motions to resolve, which statistics to predict, and which missing effects to model. It must also establish how much confidence the final number deserves.

Lesson 1 supplies the applications, prediction and uncertainty discussion, simulation families, mathematical context, physical characteristics, and repeated dye-drop example. All numerical cases below are constructed for teaching. The source's graph values are not inferred from speech. Historical quotations and nontechnical humor are omitted because their original wording and attribution have not been independently verified.

1.1 Specify the prediction before choosing a model L1 · 00:15

Let \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^3\) denote a position in the fluid, measured in metres, and let \(t\) denote time in seconds. Velocity \(\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)\in\mathbb R^3\), in m/s, assigns a motion to each position and time. Pressure \(p(\mathbf x,t)\), in Pa, contributes force per area. For a planar channel cross section \(A\), let \(|A|\) denote its area in m², \(\mathbf n\) its downstream unit normal, and \(dA\) a surface-area element in m². The volume flow rate is \(Q(t)=\int_A\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n\,dA\), in m³/s. This integral can be steady on average even when individual velocities fluctuate strongly.

For steady incompressible flow between two sections, a pressure difference \(\Delta p=p_{\rm in}-p_{\rm out}\) supplies pressure work at rate \(\Delta p\,Q\). To derive it, pressure force on a uniform section is pressure times area; multiplying by the normal speed and integrating gives \(pQ\). Incompressibility and impermeable sidewalls give the same \(Q\) at inlet and outlet, so subtracting their pressure-work rates gives the stated result. A fan's electrical power is larger when its overall efficiency is below one. Changes in kinetic energy, elevation, or heat exchange must be included when the full energy balance requires them.

As a design target, take a channel of full height \(0.04\) m and width \(1\) m, with bulk speed \(U_b=10\) m/s. Bulk speed means \(Q/|A|\), so \(|A|=0.04\) m² and \(Q=0.4\) m³/s. If the predicted pressure difference over its ten-metre length is \(100\) Pa, the corresponding pressure-work rate is \(40\) W. At an assumed fan efficiency \(\eta=0.6\), the estimated electrical requirement is \(40/0.6=66.7\) W. These numbers define a hypothetical decision, not a measured channel solution.

A pressure prediction that is twenty percent low gives a pressure-work estimate twenty percent low at fixed target \(Q\). The engineering consequence exists even if the plotted velocity field looks plausible. A model must therefore be assessed against the requested quantity, its tolerance, and the operating conditions. Correctly predicting one mean speed does not establish correct wall force, mixing, or unsteady loads.

The same distinction appears across the source's applications. Particle dispersion requires how fluid motion changes particle trajectories and concentration. A reacting flow needs the coupling between mixing and reaction, not only mean momentum. Atmospheric transport may require large-scale circulation and small-scale mixing over very different lengths. The later mathematics begins with a constant-density Newtonian fluid. Its viscous stress is linear in the symmetric velocity gradient under the stated isotropic material assumptions. This specialization does not automatically supply particle, chemical, or compressible-flow closures.

1.2 Separate physical uncertainty from numerical error L1 · 04:30

A computed number combines several modeling and numerical choices. Geometry approximates a physical domain, and boundary conditions specify how it is driven. A constitutive law describes material response, such as the relation between viscous stress and velocity gradient. A turbulence closure supplies relations for missing statistical influences in terms of the retained variables. A discrete method replaces the resulting differential equations by equations with finitely many unknowns. Stopping repeated numerical updates introduces iteration error; finite arithmetic and finite sampling can add further error. Agreement between two computations can occur because both share an inadequate model. Disagreement can also occur before any difference in physical modeling is relevant.

NASA's verification assessment addresses whether equations and their numerical solution are implemented correctly. Its validation assessment compares a model's predictions with physical evidence for its intended use. These are different questions. A converged numerical solution may accurately solve equations whose closure is unsuitable for the flow.

Let \(q\) denote the predicted pressure difference, in Pa, and let \(H\) be a dimensionless mesh spacing normalized by a fixed physical length. A constructed convergence sequence has form \(q_H=q_0+CH^2\), where \(q_0\) is the continuum prediction of the chosen model and \(C\) has pressure units. Suppose model A has \(q_0=80\) Pa and model B has \(q_0=100\) Pa, with \(C=400\) Pa for each. For mesh resolutions \(N=10,20,40\), take \(H=1/N\):

Refinement reduces numerical error in both sequences. It does not remove their twenty-pascal difference. If a reference value is stipulated to be \(100\) Pa for this manufactured example, model A converges to the wrong physical target. The reference is known by construction, not obtained from an experiment. Real validation must include uncertainty in measured data and in the conditions being reproduced.

The continuum value can be recovered algebraically under the assumed error law. Since \(q_{H/2}=q_0+CH^2/4\), multiplying by four and subtracting \(q_H\) gives

\[ q_0={4q_{H/2}-q_H\over3}. \tag{1.1} \]

Using \(N=20,40\) returns \(80\) Pa for A and \(100\) Pa for B. This extrapolation is conditional on the same leading second-order error regime at both resolutions. Oscillatory convergence, inadequate statistical sampling, changing wall treatment, or a different leading error law can invalidate it. The calculation teaches the separation of errors; it is not a grid study of an actual CFD solver.

The source shows differing predictions from two solvers. Speech alone does not identify their complete grids, discretizations, convergence criteria, or model variants. The defensible inference is that a model name does not uniquely specify an implementation. Comparing codes requires matching the actual defining equations, constants, boundary conditions, and numerical procedures.

1.3 Decide what is resolved L1 · 10:02

An eddy is a fluid motion associated with a range of spatial and temporal scales, often with rotational structure. It is not a rigid object whose boundary is always uniquely identifiable. Resolving a motion means representing its relevant variation on the computational grid and time steps with sufficiently controlled error. Modeling it means replacing some of its influence by a relation involving retained variables.

ApproachRetained descriptionMissing information or main cost
Direct numerical simulation (DNS)Instantaneous motion across all dynamically important turbulent scalesFine spatial and temporal resolution, and enough time or realizations for statistics
Large eddy simulation (LES)Spatially filtered, time-dependent velocityUnresolved-scale stresses plus sensitivity to filter, grid, numerics, and walls
Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS)Mean fields and selected statistical variablesRelations for turbulent momentum flux and any transported statistical budgets
Hybrid RANS–LESDifferent retained descriptions across regions or scalesConsistent coupling, transition of resolution, and both component models' limitations

DNS avoids an explicit turbulence closure for resolved Newtonian motion, but not boundary-data uncertainty, finite discretization, or sampling error. LES retains time dependence without retaining all turbulent scales. A RANS calculation can itself have a time-dependent mean; averaging does not necessarily mean a steady solution. A hybrid method must transfer information between its descriptions rather than merely combine their names.

Computational cost is meaningful only after specifying the target and error controls. A poorly resolved LES can be less useful than a carefully validated RANS calculation for a particular mean quantity. Resolving more scales provides additional information when the numerical and statistical requirements are met; it is not an unconditional ordering of delivered accuracy. The Turbulence Modeling Resource provides model definitions, verification cases, and fundamental validation cases while explicitly limiting how far those cases establish adequacy for complex applications.

For the channel design, one can first request mean wall force and flow rate. A later requirement for fluctuating wall loads changes the information required from the calculation. Selection follows that requirement and available validation evidence. No universal closure can be assumed to remain adequate across separation, strong rotation, particle coupling, or chemical reaction simply because it worked in an attached single-phase boundary layer.

1.4 Predictability and mathematical existence are different questions L1 · 12:18

A deterministic model assigns evolution to specified initial and boundary data, when a suitable solution exists and is unique. It may still amplify small changes in those data. Uniqueness asks whether identical complete data permit more than one solution in a stated class. Sensitivity compares different data. Observing two dye drops follow different paths addresses preparation variability and sensitivity, not a failure of same-data uniqueness.

A small discrete example makes the distinction explicit. Let the nonnegative integer \(n\) count updates, and let \(z_n\in[0,1)\) be the dimensionless state after update \(n\). The deterministic rule is \(z_{n+1}=2z_n-\lfloor2z_n\rfloor\). The floor function gives the greatest integer no larger than its argument, so the rule doubles the state and wraps it into the unit interval. Two nearby initial states differ by twice as much at the next update, provided the comparison does not cross a wrap boundary. After eight such doublings, an initial difference \(10^{-6}\) becomes \(2^8\,10^{-6}=0.000256\).

Every update is uniquely defined. Amplification of a preparation error does not contradict that property. This map is a teaching model, not a fluid or a proof of turbulent dynamics. Its role is to prevent a logical confusion before interpreting complicated fluid histories. A statistical prediction can remain useful when the exact phase or trajectory becomes difficult to predict.

The official Clay problem statement by Charles Fefferman, pp. 1–3, specifies incompressible three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations, smooth divergence-free initial data, and precise whole-space or periodic conditions. Its global smoothness/existence alternatives are not a request for an elementary closed-form formula for every engineering flow. Local smooth-solution results and weak-solution results do not by themselves settle that global question. A numerical run also cannot prove a statement about every admissible datum and arbitrarily long time.

The engineering task is more limited: formulate a physical model, resolve its relevant numerical errors, and assess predictions under specified conditions. This can be useful without resolving the global mathematical problem. Conversely, a general existence theorem would not automatically give the channel's pressure drop at an affordable computational cost.

1.5 What the physical characteristics mean L1 · 18:58

An irregular trace is not a sufficient definition of turbulence. A fluctuating stock index and a velocity measurement can both look erratic, but only the latter describes fluid motion. Even an irregular fluid signal can contain imposed forcing, instrument noise, or an evolving coherent motion. Classification needs physical context and more than the appearance of one plotted variable.

Vorticity is the curl of velocity, \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u\), in s⁻¹. For Cartesian coordinates \(x,y,z\), its first component is \(\omega_x=\partial u_z/\partial y-\partial u_y/\partial z\), with cyclic expressions for the others. It measures local rotational motion. A simple shear \(\mathbf u=(Sy,0,0)\), with constant shear rate \(S\in\mathbb R\), in s⁻¹, has \(\boldsymbol\omega=(0,0,-S)\), despite having an orderly velocity field. Nonzero vorticity alone therefore does not establish turbulence. Three-dimensional fluctuating vorticity is an important feature of ordinary three-dimensional turbulence, but an arbitrarily small coherent three-dimensional disturbance can have it too.

Turbulent transport moves momentum, heat, and species through fluctuating motion. It differs from molecular diffusion even when an averaged transport law resembles diffusion. Imagine two equally frequent exchanges across a plane: slower fluid moves toward a faster region, and faster fluid moves toward a slower region. Mean crossing velocity can vanish while momentum exchange remains nonzero. The statistical correlation between direction and transported quantity matters; the mean motion alone can omit it.

Dissipation converts mechanical energy into internal energy through molecular action. Turbulence commonly transfers energy from motions at larger scales toward smaller scales where molecular gradients are important. The transfer across scales and final dissipation are different processes. A visible eddy need not lose its energy directly to viscosity at its own scale. Flow geometry, forcing, and dimension affect the scale-transfer picture; it is not a claim that every instantaneous transfer goes in one direction.

Anisotropy means directional dependence of specified statistics. A velocity fluctuation is the deviation of velocity from its mean. The covariance matrix contains the averages of products of these fluctuations, one entry for each pair of directions. If the average squared streamwise fluctuation exceeds the wall-normal one, this matrix is anisotropic. Isotropy means invariance under rotations, a much stronger condition than equality of two plotted components. Walls select a normal direction and often produce pronounced anisotropy. Ideal homogeneous isotropic turbulence remains a useful possible statistical model; anisotropy is not a universal condition defining every turbulent flow.

Homogeneity concerns translation, not rotation: specified statistics are unchanged when the observation location is shifted within the domain under consideration. Stationarity concerns time shifts. These are separate assumptions. A statistically stationary channel can be homogeneous along its walls and inhomogeneous across them. It can have time-independent statistics while instantaneous parcels and eddies continue moving.

The Reynolds number \(Re=UL/\nu\) is dimensionless, with selected velocity scale \(U\gt0\), in m/s, length scale \(L\gt0\), in metres, and positive kinematic viscosity \(\nu\), in m²/s. Comparing advective acceleration \(U^2/L\) with molecular viscous acceleration \(\nu U/L^2\) gives their ratio \(UL/\nu\). The chosen scales must be declared. For air with \(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\) m²/s, the channel's target bulk speed and full height give \(Re=26666.7\). This calculation is a scale comparison, not a universal transition criterion.

1.6 Repeated preparations and sustained energy L1 · 25:01

Dropping dye into two nominally identical beakers can reveal different evolving structures. Drop size, release velocity, height, ambient disturbance, and concentration may differ slightly. Comparing repetitions therefore requires a specification of what is controlled and what is sampled. The fact that both contain the same fluid does not make their initial fields identical. The source's simulation comparison also changes initial conditions; different histories are compatible with a deterministic law.

An ensemble is a collection of realizations under a specified preparation law. Statistical reproducibility concerns agreement of quantities computed over that law, rather than pixel-by-pixel agreement of individual dye patterns. In the channel, repeated preparations might have different instantaneous vortices while yielding the same mean wall force within sampling uncertainty. Finite samples still produce uncertain estimates. The next chapter develops that uncertainty rather than treating the word “average” as a guarantee.

A minimal energy model illustrates why sustained fluctuations need an energy supply. Let \(e(t)\ge0\) represent mechanical fluctuation energy per unit mass, in m²/s². Let \(\mathcal I(t)\) be input per unit mass per time, in m²/s³, and let \(T\gt0\) be a prescribed decay time in seconds. Consider the deliberately simplified balance

\[ {de\over dt}=\mathcal I(t)-{e\over T}. \tag{1.2} \]

This is a phenomenological scalar model with no spatial transport, not a general turbulence dissipation closure. After switching off input at \(t=0\), separate variables: \(de/e=-dt/T\). Integration from \(e_0=e(0)\gt0\) gives \(\log(e/e_0)=-t/T\), hence \(e=e_0\exp(-t/T)\). The half-life is \(T\log2\). With \(e_0=0.5\) m²/s² and \(T=2\) s, energy after four seconds is \(0.0676676\) m²/s².

Under constant positive input \(\mathcal I_0\), equilibrium is \(e_*=\mathcal I_0T\). Writing \(r=e-e_*\) gives \(\dot r=-r/T\), so \(e=e_*+(e_0-e_*)\exp(-t/T)\). This model explains a balance between input and loss, but its exponential decay law follows from the assumed linear loss. Actual turbulent decay depends on evolving scales and need not follow that law. An energy example is useful only when its assumptions remain visible.

1.7 Execute the constructed checks

The program takes fixed design targets, a manufactured convergence sequence, a deterministic map, and the scalar energy model. It outputs power, extrapolated pressure, perturbation separation, and decay. It makes no file or network changes and raises an assertion failure when an independent formula disagrees. Its outputs are analytic checks of teaching constructions, not CFD or experimental validation. Save or run code/ch01_prediction.py with the reference Python 3.13 interpreter.

"""Constructed checks of prediction, convergence, sensitivity, and energy."""

from math import exp, floor, isclose, log

height, width, bulk_speed = 0.04, 1.0, 10.0
flow_rate = height * width * bulk_speed
pressure, efficiency = 100.0, 0.6
power = pressure * flow_rate
print(f"flow m3/s: {flow_rate:.6f}")
print(f"pressure work W: {power:.6f}")
print(f"electrical power W: {power / efficiency:.6f}")
for continuum in [80.0, 100.0]:
    values = [continuum + 400 / n**2 for n in [10, 20, 40]]
    extrapolated = (4 * values[2] - values[1]) / 3
    assert isclose(extrapolated, continuum)
    print(
        f"continuum {continuum:.0f} Pa: {values}, extrapolated "
        f"{extrapolated:.6f}"
    )
z, other, initial_gap = 0.125, 0.125001, 1e-6
for _ in range(8):
    z = 2 * z - floor(2 * z)
    other = 2 * other - floor(2 * other)
gap = abs(z - other)
assert isclose(gap, 2**8 * initial_gap, rel_tol=1e-8)
initial_energy, decay_time, time = 0.5, 2.0, 4.0
energy = initial_energy * exp(-time / decay_time)
half_time = decay_time * log(2)
assert isclose(initial_energy * exp(-half_time / decay_time), 0.25)
print(f"separation after 8 updates: {gap:.8f}")
print(f"energy at 4 s m2/s2: {energy:.8f}")
print(f"half-life s: {half_time:.6f}")

The extrapolation distinguishes numerical convergence from agreement with the stipulated reference. The map distinguishes deterministic evolution from sensitivity. The energy check tests the exact solution of the assumed scalar balance. Their different roles should not be merged into a single claim that the channel model has been validated.

1.8 Problems and worked solutions

1. Power and uncertainty. At fixed flow rate \(Q=0.4\) m³/s, compare electrical power estimates for pressure differences \(80\) and \(100\) Pa, with fan efficiency \(0.6\). Which assumptions make the pressure-to-power percentage transfer valid?

Solution

Pressure-work rates are \(80(0.4)=32\) W and \(100(0.4)=40\) W. Divide by efficiency to obtain \(53.3333\) W and \(66.6667\) W. The low estimate is twenty percent below the higher one. This proportionality holds because the flow rate and efficiency are fixed and only pressure work is being compared. If the pressure change also changes the operating flow or efficiency, those dependencies must enter before transferring a pressure percentage to electrical power.

2. Extrapolation with a different order. Suppose \(q_H=q_0+CH^p\), with known dimensionless order \(p\gt0\). Derive an expression for \(q_0\) using spacings \(H,H/2\). Why would assuming \(p=2\) without evidence be risky?

Solution

At half spacing, \(q_{H/2}=q_0+CH^p/2^p\). Multiply by \(2^p\) and subtract \(q_H\), giving \((2^p-1)q_0=2^pq_{H/2}-q_H\). Thus \(q_0=(2^pq_{H/2}-q_H)/(2^p-1)\). The formula removes the stated leading error exactly, but a wrong exponent leaves a residual bias. Multiple resolutions can help assess an asymptotic regime; finite sampling and changing boundary treatment can obscure it. Extrapolation does not remove closure error.

3. Rotation without turbulent disorder. For \(\mathbf u=(Sy,0,0)\), calculate divergence and vorticity. Which inference about turbulence is justified by these quantities alone?

Solution

Divergence is \(\partial_x(Sy)+\partial_y0+\partial_z0=0\). The curl is \((0,0,\partial_x0-\partial_ySy)=(0,0,-S)\). Thus a solenoidal orderly shear has nonzero vorticity. Nonzero vorticity alone is insufficient to classify turbulence. These expressions also do not prove that every perturbation to this field decays; stability requires a specified domain, boundary conditions, parameters, and perturbation analysis.

4. Input and decay. In (1.2), set \(T=2\) s, \(\mathcal I_0=0.1\) m²/s³, and \(e_0=0\). Find equilibrium energy and energy at two seconds. Then switch off input and determine the time required to halve the energy present at switch-off.

Solution

Equilibrium is \(e_*=\mathcal I_0T=0.2\) m²/s². The solution is \(e=0.2(1-\exp(-t/2))\), giving \(e(2)=0.1264241\) m²/s². After input is removed, use that attained energy as the new initial value and evolve exponentially with the same assumed \(T\). Halving requires \(\exp(-\Delta t/T)=1/2\), so \(\Delta t=T\log2=1.386294\) s. Independence of this half-time from starting energy is a property of the linear-loss model, not a general result about turbulent decay.

5. Same-data uniqueness. Two simulations use the same geometry and viscosity but different initial velocity fields. Their histories separate. Does that establish nonuniqueness of the governing initial-value problem? What comparison would the mathematical question require?

Solution

No. Geometry and viscosity do not specify complete initial and boundary data. The histories solve different initial-value problems. Same-data uniqueness asks whether two solutions in a stated function class can have identical full data and still differ. Numerical differences also require control of approximation error before supporting an analytical conclusion. The example can motivate sensitivity and statistical comparison, but cannot establish mathematical nonuniqueness.

1.9 Boundaries of the prediction

The channel calculation converts an assumed pressure prediction into a design consequence. The convergence sequences are manufactured, the map is discrete, and the energy equation is a scalar teaching model. None produces a validated turbulent channel profile. The physical distinctions they expose remain useful: quantity of interest versus trajectory, covariance transport versus mean crossing, discretization error versus closure error, and sensitivity versus same-data uniqueness.

The source introduces many applications, but the following core development specializes to constant-density Newtonian motion. Multiphase, reacting, compressible, and biological applications require additional assumptions and evidence. A mean prediction must be accompanied by its operating conditions, averaging rule, numerical checks, and validation scope. The reader can now specify what a model is being asked to predict and identify what kind of evidence would make its answer credible.
