13. Wall functions and smooth-wall limits

A wall function replaces an unresolved near-wall relation with a model. A wall-resolved RANS calculation instead places unknowns close enough to the wall to represent the viscous region, while still modeling the turbulent correlations. Neither approach is specified by a grid size alone. The governing closure, the meaning of its variables and the boundary treatment must agree. We will infer wall traction from a logarithmic relation, then derive the smooth-wall constraints that an integrated model should respect.

The same channel, two different near-wall problems. Keep a smooth stationary impermeable wall, constant density and incompressible Newtonian motion. Compare a first unknown in an assumed logarithmic region with one in the viscous region. The first problem supplies a modeled momentum flux. The second exposes unequal fluctuation orders and a finite dissipation that a scalar eddy viscosity cannot fully represent.

Lessons 40–43 motivate wall functions, construct equilibrium values at a first point, introduce preferential wall-normal suppression, and derive Taylor expansions for term-by-term model comparisons. Instructor DNS plots motivate the discussion but have not been digitized here. The construction below corrects stress signs, logarithm constants, the distinction between a wall relation and independent boundary values, and overbroad claims about isotropy or exceptions to no-slip kinematics.

13.1 Decide which near-wall region the first unknown represents L40 · 00:14

Let \(y\geq0\) be distance normal to a flat wall, \(U\) mean tangential velocity relative to that wall, \(\rho\gt 0\) density and \(\nu\gt 0\) molecular kinematic viscosity. For positive tangential wall traction magnitude \(\tau_w\), define \(u_\tau=\sqrt{\tau_w/\rho}\), \(y^+=yu_\tau/\nu\), \(U^+=U/u_\tau\). Our derivations use a fixed direction of positive shear. At zero traction these scales degenerate; reversed or multidirectional traction needs a corresponding vector and sign treatment.

The physical no-slip condition is \(U(0)=0\). Under smooth-wall regularity the mean velocity begins linearly, \(U^+\sim y^+\). An empirical logarithmic description farther away is \(U^+=(1/\kappa)\ln y^++B\), where \(\kappa,B\) are fit constants and \(\ln\) denotes the natural logarithm. The lecture uses values near \(0.41\) and \(5.2\) or \(5.5\), rather than claiming a universally exact intercept. Between these regimes lies a buffer region in which neither limiting expression alone describes the full profile.

A classical equilibrium wall-function calculation places its first representative point \(P\), at distance \(y_P\), in the region where its chosen logarithmic relation is credible. The lecture gives illustrative guidance \(y_P^+\gtrsim30\), often a range around 30–100 with an upper limit depending on the flow's available scale separation. These are not universal bounds. There must actually be space between the viscous region and outer development, and pressure-gradient, curvature, roughness or separation effects may invalidate the assumed relation.

A wall-resolved low-Reynolds-number RANS treatment instead commonly targets a first point near \(y_P^+\lesssim1\) and several points through the viscous and buffer region. Lesson 43 suggests roughly 5–10 points below \(y^+\approx20\). Such numbers express resolution intent, not a proof of convergence. Cell-centre distance, full first-cell thickness and adjacent point spacing are different quantities: on an orthogonal cell-centred mesh next to a plane wall, the first centre is typically half the first cell thickness away. A vertex scheme has another arrangement. Report the actual quantity used.

“High” and “low” Reynolds number in this discussion refer to the local wall treatment. A globally high-Reynolds-number channel still contains a region in which molecular viscosity matters. Conversely, a large global Reynolds number does not make every first point lie in a valid logarithmic region. Moving the first point while holding an unmodified wall function fixed can move it outside that function's assumptions.

A mesh coordinate is not a known wall coordinate

With \(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}\) and a first centre at \(y_P=2\ \mathrm{mm}\), \(u_\tau=0.2,0.4,0.8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) gives \(y_P^+=26.6667,53.3333,106.6667\). The same physical mesh spans different regions as traction changes. Since traction is usually part of the computed result, a preliminary estimate must be checked afterward across the wall.

For \(u_\tau=0.4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(y^+=1\) corresponds to \(37.5\ \mu\mathrm m\), whereas \(y^+=50\) corresponds to \(1.875\ \mathrm{mm}\). This difference explains the cost motivation in the source's complicated-geometry examples. It does not establish that a simple smooth-wall law remains accurate around every object in such a geometry.

13.2 A log law relates velocity and traction implicitly L41 · 00:15

Introduce a positive dimensionless constant \(E=\exp(\kappa B)\). Then the same fitted law is

\[ \frac{U_P}{u_\tau}=\frac1\kappa\ln\left(E\frac{y_Pu_\tau}{\nu}\right), \qquad F(u_\tau)=\frac{u_\tau}{\kappa} \ln\left(E\frac{y_Pu_\tau}{\nu}\right)-U_P=0. \tag{13.1} \]

Here \(U_P\gt 0\) is the wall-relative tangential velocity at the first point. The logarithm has a dimensionless argument. A letter \(E\) in this formula is a fitted factor, not the mathematical base \(e\). For \(\kappa=0.41,B=5.2\), \(E=8.43171339\). The lecture's illustrative \(E=9\) instead corresponds to \(B=\ln9/0.41=5.35908433\). Replacing \(B\) by a logarithm preserves a fit only when these constants satisfy that relation.

Equation (13.1) is one relation between a first-point velocity and traction. In a coupled finite-volume calculation, the first-cell velocity can remain a momentum unknown while a wall function supplies the associated face stress. An alternative boundary placement can use the relation to prescribe a value at a truncated domain boundary. One should not independently prescribe \(U_P\), \(\tau_w\) and the same wall law as three unrelated pieces of data. The lecture's language of “giving values at \(P\)” is a schematic closure construction; the discrete implementation must specify which quantities are unknown.

A concrete primary example is the Foundation 4.1 velocity-based wall-function implementation. Its inspected functions use wall-adjacent relative velocity to calculate \(y^+\) and a patch turbulent viscosity. Thus wall treatment can enter through a stress coefficient rather than overwriting every first-cell velocity. We use that implementation only to establish this distinction; our bracketed Python calculation below is original and is not a reproduction or execution of the CFD code.

To solve (13.1), set \(a=Ey_P/\nu\gt 0\). On the branch \(a u_\tau\geq1\), \(F'(u_\tau)=[\ln(a u_\tau)+1]/\kappa>0\). At \(u_\tau=1/a\), \(F=-U_P<0\); for sufficiently large \(u_\tau\), \(F>0\). There is exactly one root on this increasing branch. Bisection with an expanding upper bracket is consequently reliable for this restricted equation. A root of the algebraic relation still needs an a posteriori check that its \(y_P^+\) lies in the intended physical region.

Convergence of the wall-law equation does not establish its applicability

For \(U_P=8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(y_P=0.002\ \mathrm m\), the constants above and \(\rho=1.2\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), bisection gives \(u_\tau=0.51553643\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(y_P^+=68.73819102\), and \(\tau_w=0.31893338\ \mathrm{Pa}\). The residual is below \(10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). These values are consistent with the chosen log-region range; they remain conditional estimates under the wall-law assumptions.

For a controlled test, choose a known \(u_\tau=0.4\) and manufacture a velocity from the same log law at \(y_P^+=50\). The inference recovers \(0.4\) to roundoff. Now place the point at true \(y_P^+=1\) and manufacture its velocity from the linear law \(U_P^+=1\). Forcing that viscous sample into the logarithmic inference gives \(u_\tau=0.14594127\), about 63.5% below the known value. The root converges in both tests; the second uses an inappropriate constitutive relation. These are manufactured law checks, not measurements of a particular DNS profile.

13.3 Equilibrium wall values inherit the same assumptions L41 · 12:59

Let \(R_{ij}=\mathbb E[u_i'u_j']\) be the velocity covariance, \(k=R_{ii}/2\), and take \(x,y,z\) tangential, normal and spanwise coordinates. In positive simple shear, \(R_{12}\) is usually negative. A turbulent shear contribution to positive total stress is \(-\rho R_{12}\), not \(+\rho R_{12}\). In a constant-stress region where molecular shear is negligible, \(-R_{12}\approx u_\tau^2\). If the calibrated ratio \(-R_{12}/k\approx\sqrt{C_\mu}\) also applies, then

\[ k_P\approx\frac{u_\tau^2}{\sqrt{C_\mu}},\qquad \epsilon_P\approx\mathcal P_P =\frac{u_\tau^3}{\kappa y_P},\qquad \omega_P=\frac{\epsilon_P}{\beta_* k_P} =\frac{u_\tau\sqrt{C_\mu}}{\beta_*\kappa y_P}. \tag{13.2} \]

The destruction-rate estimate uses local equilibrium and \(U'=u_\tau/(\kappa y)\), with constant turbulent stress. When \(\beta_*=C_\mu\), the last expression reduces to \(u_\tau/(\sqrt{C_\mu}\kappa y_P)\). All three fields are estimates in an assumed region, rather than the exact solid-wall values. In particular a positive \(k_P\) far from the wall does not contradict the exact \(k(0)=0\).

The source mentions conventions that omit \(\beta_*\) from the definition of \(\omega\). If a new variable is \(\widetilde\omega=\epsilon/k=\beta_*\omega\), it has the same units but a different normalization. Viscosity becomes \(\nu_t=\beta_* k/\widetilde\omega\) when the original relation is \(k/\omega\); its source and boundary coefficients must be transformed too. Removing a constant only from a boundary formula while retaining every original equation is a model change, not a harmless renaming. We do not infer a particular commercial implementation from this caption discussion.

For the inferred traction above with \(C_\mu=\beta_*=0.09\), (13.2) gives \(k_P=0.88592604\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\), \(\epsilon_P=167.09529977\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-3}}\), \(\omega_P=2095.67655558\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). These values close the selected equilibrium construction. They need not be valid in a rapidly developing or separated near-wall flow where transport and production–destruction imbalance matter.

A discrete stress calculation also clarifies why “wall viscosity” is not necessarily the physical limiting turbulent viscosity. If a one-sided approximation uses the coarse gradient \(U_P/y_P\), the effective coefficient needed to give the modeled traction is \(\nu_{\rm eff,w}=u_\tau^2 y_P/U_P\). A patch adjustment is \(\nu_{t,w}=\nu_{\rm eff,w}-\nu\). In wall variables, this is

\[ \frac{\nu_{t,w}}{\nu} =\frac{y_P^+}{U_P^+}-1 =\frac{\kappa y_P^+}{\ln(Ey_P^+)}-1. \]

The example gives \(\nu_{\rm eff,w}=0.00006644\) and \(\nu_{t,w}=0.00005144\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}\). This coefficient compensates for a gradient spanning an unresolved layer. It should not be interpreted as the actual turbulent fluctuation transport at the no-slip wall, where the covariance vanishes. If the wall function or discretization uses another stress construction, this algebra must be adapted to it.

13.4 The two-component limit concerns relative covariance L42 · 00:15

Lesson 42 shows that wall-normal fluctuations are suppressed differently from tangential ones. The limiting statement should be precise. At the wall all three velocity fluctuations vanish under no slip. “Two-component” therefore does not mean two nonzero fluctuating velocities remain exactly on the wall. It describes the limiting normalized covariance as the wall is approached: the normal variance becomes negligible relative to the sum of tangential variances, provided that sum has a nonzero leading coefficient.

Pressure is determined nonlocally by incompressibility and momentum together with boundary conditions. Its redistribution and transport help constrain component energies near a wall. Viscosity also damps gradients. These mechanisms cannot be separated into a universal rule that pressure acts only on the normal component and viscosity only on tangential components. Pressure–strain redistributes energy between components with zero total trace in incompressible flow; the scalar \(k\) equation loses that trace-free information.

The source's approximate distances around \(y^+\lesssim200\) for wall-normal influence and \(y^+\lesssim20\) for strong viscous effects refer to its physical discussion and examples, not universal onset locations. Rotation and stratification can change stresses and layer organization. They do not by themselves invalidate the local no-slip and incompressible-continuity Taylor argument at a smooth impermeable wall. If regularity, permeability or compressibility changes, the kinematic argument needs to be redone.

Three modeling responses appear in the source. A low-Reynolds-number eddy-viscosity treatment changes near-wall coefficients or terms while retaining a scalar stress closure. A \(v^2\)–\(f\) family introduces a component-related energy and an elliptic field so wall influence can enter its viscosity construction. The inspected Foundation 5 model declaration identifies \(f\) as an elliptic damping field: four equations should not all be called time-dependent transport equations. A Reynolds-stress model transports the six independent covariances and closes pressure–strain and other correlations. Having those equations creates the capacity to represent anisotropy; it does not ensure that every chosen pressure–strain closure gives the right wall limit.

In parallel mean shear, the linear Boussinesq relation \(R_{ij}^{m}=2k\delta_{ij}/3-2\nu_t S_{ij}\) predicts \(R_{11}^{m}=R_{22}^{m}=R_{33}^{m}=2k/3\), because its diagonal strain entries vanish. Damping the scalar \(\nu_t\) changes the shear stress but does not remove these equal modeled normal stresses. Nor does the scalar definition of \(k\) itself impose isotropy on the actual field. This structural restriction is why an energy equation and an improved wall viscosity still cannot supply all component information.

13.5 Derive the wall orders before comparing model terms L43 · 00:15

Let the instantaneous fluctuations be sufficiently differentiable near a fixed flat wall, with the needed moments finite and derivatives commuting with expectation. Expand at fixed \(x,z,t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} u'&=a_0+a_1y+a_2y^2+\cdots,\\ v'&=b_0+b_1y+b_2y^2+\cdots,\\ w'&=c_0+c_1y+c_2y^2+\cdots . \end{aligned} \]

Coefficients depend on \(x,z,t\); Taylor factorials have been absorbed, for example \(a_2=(\partial_y^2u')_w/2\). No slip and impermeability give \(a_0=b_0=c_0=0\). Because the wall traces \(u'(x,0,z,t)\) and \(w'(x,0,z,t)\) are identically zero, their tangential derivatives are zero there. Instantaneous incompressibility then gives \((\partial_yv')_w=-(\partial_xu'+\partial_zw')_w=0\), so \(b_1=0\). Consequently \(u',w'=O(y)\), whereas \(v'=O(y^2)\).

The next continuity coefficient is also informative: \(2b_2=-(\partial_xa_1+\partial_zc_1)\). Thus the normal coefficient is constrained by spatially varying tangential wall gradients; it is not an arbitrary independent number. Squaring and averaging with suitable moment regularity gives the following leading possible terms. An indicated coefficient can vanish in a special field, so the order statement is an upper bound, not a guarantee of a nonzero amplitude.

QuantityLeading possible termOrder near the wall
\(R_{11}\)\(\mathbb E[a_1^2]y^2\)\(O(y^2)\)
\(R_{22}\)\(\mathbb E[b_2^2]y^4\)\(O(y^4)\)
\(R_{33}\)\(\mathbb E[c_1^2]y^2\)\(O(y^2)\)
\(R_{12},R_{23}\)\(\mathbb E[a_1b_2]y^3,\ \mathbb E[b_2c_1]y^3\)\(O(y^3)\)
\(R_{13}\)\(\mathbb E[a_1c_1]y^2\)\(O(y^2)\)
\(k\)\(Ay^2,\quad A=\tfrac12\mathbb E[a_1^2+c_1^2]\)\(O(y^2)\)

For \(A>0\), \(R_{22}/(2k)=O(y^2)\to0\). The normal rms velocity is \(O(y^2)\), but the normal variance is \(O(y^4)\); confusing them loses a power of two. The normalized tangential covariance need not be isotropic within its surviving plane. The limit singles out the wall-normal direction, not equal tangential fluctuation strengths.

A realizable ensemble with the required continuity constraint

In nondimensional coordinates, choose independent random signs \(a,c=\pm1\) with equal probabilities. Set \(u'=-a y\cos x\), \(v'=-a y^2\sin x/2\), \(w'=c y\). Every component has zero expectation, vanishes at \(y=0\), and \(\partial_xu'+\partial_yv'+\partial_zw'=a y\sin x-a y\sin x+0=0\). This is a kinematic construction, not an asserted Navier–Stokes solution.

Its covariances are \(R_{11}=y^2\cos^2x\), \(R_{22}=y^4\sin^2x/4\), \(R_{33}=y^2\), \(R_{12}=y^3\cos x\sin x/2\), with other off-diagonals zero. They are realizable because they come from an explicit ensemble. At \(x=1\), halving \(y\) approximately quarters \(R_{22}/(2k)\), while \(R_{12}/y^3=0.22732436\) stays constant. The sign of this illustrative covariance need not match a turbulent channel: kinematic admissibility alone does not impose a turbulent energy-transfer direction.

13.6 A finite wall dissipation balances molecular diffusion L43 · 18:40

Use the pseudo-dissipation definition \(\epsilon=\nu\mathbb E[(\partial_j u_i')(\partial_j u_i')]\). At the smooth wall the surviving fluctuation derivatives are the normal derivatives of both tangential components. Therefore

\[ \epsilon_w=\nu\mathbb E[a_1^2+c_1^2]=2\nu A,\qquad \left.\nu\frac{d^2k}{dy^2}\right|_w=2\nu A. \tag{13.3} \]

The spanwise contribution is not generically absent. The leading \(\epsilon\) is independent of distance in the sense of a finite constant limit, not a statement that \(\epsilon(y)\) is constant throughout the layer. Similarly a finite leading mean gradient \(U'(0)\) can have higher-order distance dependence. For \(A=0.7\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), \(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\), the two rates in (13.3) both equal \(2.1\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-3}}\).

Consider a statistically stationary channel homogeneous in tangential directions. Its exact scalar energy balance is \(0=\nu k''-(T_y+q_y)'+\mathcal P-\epsilon\), with \(T_y=\tfrac12\mathbb E[v'(u'^2+v'^2+w'^2)]\), \(q_y=\mathbb E[p'v']/\rho\), and \(\mathcal P=-R_{12}U'\). The derived velocity orders give \(T_y=O(y^4)\), so \(T_y'=O(y^3)\). For finite regular pressure, \(q_y=O(y^2)\) and \(q_y'=O(y)\), possibly smaller if a coefficient vanishes. Pressure transport is not identically zero because a scalar model chose to omit it. Production is generically \(O(y^3)\) if the mean wall gradient is finite. Thus the constant leading terms are molecular diffusion and \(-\epsilon\), which cancel as (13.3) requires.

The undamped baseline viscosity has a revealing mismatch. If its \(k,\epsilon\) were given the physical limits just derived, \(\nu_t=C_\mu k^2/\epsilon\sim[C_\mu A^2/\epsilon_w]y^4\). For finite nonzero \(U'(0)\), its shear covariance \(-\nu_t U'\) would then be \(O(y^4)\), whereas the exact covariance permits a nonzero cubic term. A necessary order for matching that generic shear is \(\nu_t=O(y^3)\). This comparison does not mean multiplying the baseline formula by an arbitrary small function will solve the problem: under these specific physical variable limits, changing a quartic to a cubic leading term requires a factor proportional to \(1/y\). Some low-Reynolds-number formulations alter destruction variables or add source terms as well.

A named model must therefore be inspected as a whole. For example the documented Chien model includes additional near-wall terms and specifies its own wall data, including zero for its modeled \(\epsilon\) variable. That is not a boundary value to transplant into the unmodified physical definition in (13.3). A shared variable label does not make different modeled balances interchangeable. The next recordings develop these term-by-term corrections.

The modeled gradient flux for energy would scale as \(-(\nu_t/\sigma_k)k'=O(y^5)\) under the bare quartic estimate, compared with the exact possible \(O(y^4)\) triple flux. Both vanish at the wall, but agreeing only on a zero boundary value is weaker than matching the first nonzero behavior. Likewise getting total \(k\) approximately right cannot by itself establish the correct normal-variance fraction. These are different constraints on a near-wall model.

13.7 Reproduce the numerical and kinematic checks

Run ch13_wall_treatment.py with standard Python 3. The bracketed inference enforces a positive increasing branch, then reports the inferred wall coordinate. Its deliberately invalid viscous sample tests applicability, rather than root-finder accuracy. The random-field formulas test kinematic limits without claiming a turbulent simulation.

"""Restricted wall-law inference and smooth-wall limiting checks."""

from math import exp, log, sqrt

nu, rho, kappa, B, Cmu = 1.5e-5, 1.2, 0.41, 5.2, 0.09
E = exp(kappa * B)
print(f"logarithm factor E for B=5.2: {E:.8f}")
print(f"B implied by lecture illustrative E=9: {log(9) / kappa:.8f}")


def infer_friction(speed, distance):
    """Positive root on the increasing branch E*y*u/nu >= 1."""
    assert speed > 0 and distance > 0
    lower = nu / (E * distance)
    upper = max(speed, 2 * lower)

    def residual(value):
        return value * log(E * distance * value / nu) / kappa - speed

    while residual(upper) < 0:
        upper *= 2
    for _ in range(80):
        middle = (lower + upper) / 2
        if residual(middle) > 0:
            upper = middle
        else:
            lower = middle
    value = (lower + upper) / 2
    return value, residual(value)


speed, distance = 8.0, 0.002
ut, residual = infer_friction(speed, distance)
yp = distance * ut / nu
kp = ut**2 / sqrt(Cmu)
ep = ut**3 / (kappa * distance)
omega = ep / (Cmu * kp)
effective_viscosity = ut**2 * distance / speed
print(f"inferred friction m/s: {ut:.8f}; residual m/s: {residual:.3e}")
print(f"inferred y-plus: {yp:.8f}; wall stress Pa: {rho * ut ** 2:.8f}")
print(f"equilibrium k, epsilon, omega SI: {kp:.8f}, {ep:.8f}, {omega:.8f}")
print(f"effective wall-face viscosity m2/s: {effective_viscosity:.8f}")
print(f"wall-face turbulent adjustment m2/s: {effective_viscosity - nu:.8f}")
assert abs(residual) < 1e-12 and 30 < yp < 100

# Generate exact log samples, then move the point into the viscous region.
known = 0.4
for yplus in (1.0, 50.0):
    distance = yplus * nu / known
    sample = known * (yplus if yplus == 1 else log(E * yplus) / kappa)
    inferred, _ = infer_friction(sample, distance)
    print(
        f"manufactured y-plus={yplus:.0f}: recovered "
        f"u_tau={inferred:.8f}, relative "
        f"error={inferred / known - 1:.8f}"
    )

for trial in (0.2, 0.4, 0.8):
    print(
        f"fixed first distance2mm with u_tau={trial:.1f}: "
        f"y-plus={0.002 * trial / nu:.8f}"
    )

# Kinematic, divergence-free ensemble near a flat no-slip wall.
# u'=-a*y*cos(x), v'=-a*y^2*sin(x)/2, w'=c*y.
# a,c are independent equiprobable +/-1; zero means, not an NS solution.
for y in (0.1, 0.05, 0.025):
    x = 1.0
    from math import cos, sin

    R11 = y * y * cos(x) ** 2
    R22 = y**4 * sin(x) ** 2 / 4
    R33 = y * y
    energy = (R11 + R22 + R33) / 2
    R12 = y**3 * cos(x) * sin(x) / 2
    print(
        f"kinematic y={y:.3f}: R22/(2k)={R22 / (2 * energy):.8f}, "
        f"R12/y^3={R12 / y ** 3:.8f}"
    )

# Finite physical dissipation and molecular diffusion at the wall.
A = 0.7
eps_wall = 2 * nu * A
print(
    f"k=A*y^2 wall epsilon and molecular diffusion: {eps_wall:.8e}, "
    f"{2 * nu * A:.8e}"
)
print(f"bare k-epsilon nu_t/y^4 coefficient: {Cmu * A * A / eps_wall:.8f}")

13.8 Exercises with complete solutions

1. Convert a first-cell height to a wall coordinate without losing the factor of two.

For a plane orthogonal cell-centred mesh with first cell thickness \(h_1=100\ \mu\mathrm m\), its centre is at \(y_P=50\ \mu\mathrm m\). With \(u_\tau=0.4\), \(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\), \(y_P^+=1.333333\). Using \(h_1\) as the distance would give \(2.666667\). Neither value alone establishes convergence; the actual scheme, model and rest of the mesh matter. A first-centre target \(y_P^+=1\) here corresponds to \(h_1=75\ \mu\mathrm m\) under the same geometry assumption.

2. Is the log-law factor 9 exactly equivalent to intercept 5.2?

No. Equality requires \(E=\exp(\kappa B)\). For \(\kappa=0.41,B=5.2\), \(E=8.43171339\). Conversely \(E=9\) gives \(B=5.35908433\). They may represent nearby illustrative fits, but cannot be substituted while claiming exact algebraic equivalence. The logarithm's argument \(Ey_Pu_\tau/\nu\) is dimensionless.

3. Prove the root used by the friction inference is unique on its selected branch.

For \(a=Ey_P/\nu>0\), define \(F(s)=s\ln(as)/\kappa-U_P\). At \(s=1/a\), \(F=-U_P<0\); \(F(s)\to\infty\) as \(s\to\infty\). On \(as\geq1\), \(F'=(\ln(as)+1)/\kappa>0\). Continuity and strict monotonicity give exactly one root there. Repeated upper-bound doubling eventually brackets it, and bisection converges. This proof concerns an algebraic branch; a resulting \(y_P^+\) in the viscous region would still fail the physical assumptions of the logarithmic relation.

4. Rescale omega consistently.

Starting with \(\omega=\epsilon/(\beta_*k)\), define \(\widetilde\omega=\epsilon/k=\beta_*\omega\). Then \(\nu_t=k/\omega=\beta_*k/\widetilde\omega\). A source \(-\beta\omega^2\) in \(D_U\omega\) becomes \(-(\beta/\beta_*)\widetilde\omega^2\) in \(D_U\widetilde\omega\), because the whole equation is multiplied by \(\beta_*\). Similarly \(\widetilde\omega_P=u_\tau\sqrt{C_\mu}/(\kappa y_P)\). Keeping the original source coefficient and viscosity formula while changing only this boundary normalization gives a different system.

5. Why does the linear coefficient of the normal fluctuation vanish?

The wall traces \(u'=w'=0\) hold for every tangential position and time. Their tangential derivatives on the wall are therefore zero, assuming differentiability. Incompressibility gives \(\partial_yv'=-(\partial_xu'+\partial_zw')=0\) there. Since \(b_1=(\partial_yv')_w\), \(b_1=0\). At the next order \(2b_2=-(\partial_xa_1+\partial_zc_1)\). A body rotation or buoyancy term in momentum does not change this local continuity identity; changing permeability or the applicable continuity equation can change the argument.

6. Derive the normalized two-component limit without claiming nonzero fluctuations on the wall.

With \(k=Ay^2+O(y^3)\), \(A>0\), and \(R_{22}=By^4+O(y^5)\), the fraction \(R_{22}/(2k)=(B/(2A))y^2+O(y^3)\to0\). Yet every unnormalized variance tends to zero. The tangential fractions sum to one in the limit, with their ratio determined by \(\mathbb E[a_1^2]\) and \(\mathbb E[c_1^2]\), not forced to be equal. If \(A=0\), this division is not justified and the leading expansion must be examined again.

7. Show which finite terms cancel at the wall and identify both tangential contributions.

At the wall, all tangential derivatives of fluctuation traces vanish and \(\partial_yv'=0\). Thus the pseudo-dissipation reduces to \(\nu\mathbb E[(\partial_yu')_w^2+(\partial_yw')_w^2]=\nu\mathbb E[a_1^2+c_1^2]\). Since \(k=\mathbb E[a_1^2+c_1^2]y^2/2+\cdots\), \(\nu k''_w\) is the same number. Production and both regular transport divergences vanish in the limit under the stated stationary homogeneous channel assumptions. Discarding the spanwise derivative without an additional two-component field restriction loses part of the dissipation.

8. Can a scalar viscosity repair the normal stresses in parallel shear?

For \(U=(U(y),0,0)\), all diagonal entries of the mean strain vanish. The Boussinesq model therefore predicts three identical diagonal covariances \(2k/3\), regardless of the scalar viscosity chosen. Exact smooth-wall orders have \(R_{22}=O(y^4)\), \(R_{11},R_{33}=O(y^2)\) in a generic field. If modeled \(k\sim Ay^2\), the modeled normal fraction remains \(1/3\), while the exact fraction tends to zero. Altering a viscosity damping function alone cannot fix this tensor restriction, even if it improves the shear stress and total energy.

A near-wall correction should now be assessed by its stated variable definitions, leading balances, tensor restrictions and numerical boundary implementation. Agreement with one wall value or a converged log-law iteration is only one piece of that assessment.
